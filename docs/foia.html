Editorial · Citing Aziel, 28 July 2026

This is not freedom of information

A time-volume door. A cost door. Both leave the file on the institution’s side of the desk.

Aziel’s paper of 28 July 2026, Acknowledgement of Structural Failure in FOIA Practice, records the Freedom of Information Act as a statutory right that operational practice converts into controlled disclosure.

The paper’s subtitle names the mechanism: the time-volume / cost binary as controlled access. Two pathways, one retained file.

Mode 1 — the clock and the volume. Process the request under severe time and volume constraints and the agency denies or releases the absolute minimum that exemption language will cover. The statutory twenty working days in 5 U.S.C. § 552, with “unusual circumstances” and a further ten days, become a control surface. Volume is converted into a defense.

Mode 2 — the fee. Allow the request to expand and the agency shifts search, review, and duplication cost onto the requester until the request is financially prohibitive. The public-interest waiver exists in the text. It is not the default in the room.

Either outcome, the paper states, leaves the information under institutional control. Neither outcome constitutes freedom of information. The binary is not a rhetorical device. It is the practical operating surface of the statute as implemented.

The original author PDF was not present in this environment. A site citation transcript of the supplied page-1 arguments and the author’s summary is filed at /assets/foia-binary-acknowledgement.pdf. The statute as published by the government remains at foia.gov.

Section 8 · Closing Acknowledgement

Both pathways ration information. Control is retained. That is not freedom of information.

Aziel’s closing acknowledgement — section 8 of the 28 July 2026 paper — does not treat Mode 1 and Mode 2 as opposite remedies. They are complementary instruments of the same architecture. A denial issued under clock and volume pressure, and a fee estimate that makes production impossible, are two receipts for the same withheld record.

The acknowledgement is structural, not personal. It does not require a named villain at a desk. It requires only that the statute as implemented keep the default on the institution’s side of the door.

Volume IV · Chapter 8

Contradictory statements and the staged photograph

Volume IV states that William Nadeau provided the primary eyewitness account for the official narrative and that his statements contain multiple irreconcilable physical contradictions. It records his claims that he was standing only “one foot away,” that Zioncheck “ran around the desk,” “brushed past” him, and “dove headfirst” through the open window, and that Zioncheck went through the window in one fluid motion without waiting to see if it was open.

The same chapter examines the official death-scene photograph (WSU Libraries Digital Collections). Volume IV’s key observations: Nadeau stands in the doorway looking directly at the camera, his expression notably calm and composed, with no visible distress, shock, or grief; Zioncheck’s hat and coat are already laid out on the floor in a dramatic arrangement, contradicting accounts that Nadeau was “reaching for the hat” when Zioncheck suddenly jumped; the photo has the appearance of being taken after the fact for press purposes; and Nadeau was Rubye Nix Zioncheck’s brother-in-law.

Volume IV concludes that the image suggests the scene was managed for public consumption rather than documented in the immediate aftermath of a traumatic event.

Volume IV · Chapters 2–6

One foot away, 2.7 to 3.8 seconds

Volume IV places Nadeau’s proximity claim against a calculated movement timeline. The minimum realistic time it assigns to the described sequence — initiation, run with direction change, posture transition, and launch — is 2.7–3.8+ seconds in a cluttered fifth-floor office. In that interval, the volume argues, even moderate reaction time places a person in immediate physical-contact range well before Zioncheck could reach the window and execute the required posture drop through a 27–33 inch sash opening.

Chapter 6 repeats the cornerstone claim: Nadeau said he was standing only one foot away and “missed him by a foot.” Volume IV calls that claim irreconcilable with the documented constraints.

Volume V · Vic and the sequence

Nadeau in Vic’s account

Volume V identifies Vic (Victor Anthony / Antoni Zajaczek, maternal cousin) as the unplanned witness who, in its account, saw the body being positioned while leaving the Alaskan Cigar Store late on August 7, 1936. The chapter states that he knew William Nadeau personally, was threatened on the spot, and stayed silent. It then lists a complete archival void after that date: no marriage record, death certificate, obituary, census entry, business records, or further newspaper or directory mentions.

The same volume’s named-participants chapter describes William Nadeau as brother-in-law: helped move the body, silenced Rubye, sold the false narrative, with an account that Volume IV treats as physically contradictory.

The two-building discussion remains on the main paper: Vic’s workplace in the old Arctic Club / Morrison Hotel; Marion’s office in the newer Arctic Building.
