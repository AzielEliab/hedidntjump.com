01 / THE CASE

What happened
at the Arctic Building?

Marion Zioncheck was a U.S. congressman from Washington. His death in Seattle on August 7, 1936 was reported as suicide following a fall from his fifth-floor office.

This project challenges that account. Its collected analyses question the reported movements inside the office, the interpretation of photographs, witness accounts, and the surrounding institutional history.

01 Why was Marion sent from Washington, D.C. to Seattle, Washington?+

The contemporary account reproduced in Volume II describes Kenneth Romney, the House sergeant-at-arms, arranging safe conduct back to Seattle after Marion’s escape from the Maryland institution. It says Capitol police intervened to prevent another arrest, and that Marion ultimately traveled west by train after an airline refused him passage in Chicago. The report connects the return with his mother in Seattle and Romney’s assistance during his difficulties.

02 Why do archive records conflict with the official narrative online today?+

The archive preserves the details that the investigation compares: differing descriptions of the last movements, the photographed note, the room arrangement, and the two Arctic locations. Volume IV places those details alongside the account of a sudden fifth-floor jump. Reading the records together brings the wording, physical setting, and sequence of events into the same frame.

03 Why was Marion trying to stay in the media and spotlight?+

The August 8 report in Volume II says Marion re-entered the congressional race to show the public he was neither crazy nor foolish. That explanation makes public appearances part of his response to the image attached to him. The archive also preserves headlines centered on his behavior, while Volume V discusses his planned political speech and conflict with powerful institutions.

04 What can photographs establish?+

Photographs document visible objects and their positions at the time of exposure. Volume IV examines Nadeau’s position in the doorway and the placement of Marion’s hat and coat on the floor. It compares that arrangement with the account of Nadeau reaching for the hat as Marion moved toward the window.

05 Where are records to his “Who’s Crazy Now” speech?+

This entry centers Marion’s own public response to how he was portrayed. The supplied collection preserves newspaper accounts of his return to the race and his effort to rebut that portrayal. Those clippings provide the surrounding public context; the collection does not include a full transcript under the title “Who’s Crazy Now.”

06 Why did Marion dress up for a speech — then decide to “jump”?+

Volume V describes Marion’s commitment to speaking at the postal workers’ dinner that evening. The investigation places that planned appearance beside the reported sudden jump: he had an audience, an engagement, and a purpose for the evening. The chapter discusses the speech preparations as part of the sequence leading to the Arctic Building.

07 Why did his wife never give a statement as the most prolific witness?+

The contemporary report in Volume II places Rubye in the car just feet from where Marion’s body landed, then describes her collapse and removal to a hospital. Its account of her experience is narrated by the report rather than presented as a detailed first-person statement from her. Volume V makes her proximity, subsequent actions, and relationship with the Nadeaus central to its discussion of the human evidence.

08 Why are the injuries inconsistent with a five-story fall?+

Volume IV argues that the described injury distribution fits ground-level restraint and blunt-force trauma more closely than the reported head-first fall. Its comparison focuses on cranial trauma, the neck, the limbs, and the broader pattern of injuries it expects from a fall of that height. This is the injury-pattern argument developed in the physics volume.

Volume IV · PDF p. 10 ↗
09 Why were so few witnesses named — and why was one of them the person who broke the story?+

Volume V places Sol Krems in two roles: a named witness at the scene and the newspaper figure it credits with the first story. It contrasts that named role with references to scores of unnamed witnesses. The chapter follows the overlap between the sidewalk account and the narrative that reached readers the next morning.

Volume V · PDF p. 6 ↗
10 How and why did the record get meshed between the old Arctic building at 501 3rd Avenue and the new Arctic building at 3rd Avenue and Cherry Street?+

Volume IV identifies two distinct locations: the old Arctic Club / Morrison Hotel at 501 3rd Avenue, associated with Vic’s Alaskan Cigar Store, and the newer Arctic Building at 3rd Avenue and Cherry Street, where Marion’s fifth-floor office was located. The volume argues that merging those locations moved the final conversation into the death-scene building and compressed the sequence into a spontaneous-jump narrative.

11 Where are his dinner-party speech papers?+

Volume V describes Marion returning to the Arctic Building with part of a speech or letter missing and identifies House stationery in its account of that writing. The dinner speech papers concern what he intended to say to the postal workers’ audience. Their content and disposition are central to the chapter’s account of his preparations that evening.

12 Why does his “suicide note” read like part of a speech — and why was it folded if he had just written it?+

Volume II reproduces the handwritten note on House of Representatives stationery, with visible horizontal and vertical creases. Its text concerns an unfair economic system and the chance to survive and live. That political language connects this entry to the dinner speech discussed in Volume V. The photographed paper and its creases also belong beside the account in Volume I that Nadeau found Marion writing shortly before the reported jump.

13 Where did cousin “Vic” go after that fateful day?+

Volume V identifies Vic as Marion’s maternal cousin and describes a break in the record after August 7, 1936. Its chapter lists marriage, death, obituary, census, business, newspaper, and directory records as the areas in which the investigation found no subsequent trace. Vic’s family connection and disappearance from the assembled record are the focus of that chapter.

Volume V · PDF p. 5 ↗
14 Why was “Vic” there that night when he worked in the opposite Arctic building?+

The volumes place Vic’s workplace in the old Arctic Club / Morrison Hotel and Marion’s office in the newer Arctic Building. Volume V describes Vic leaving the cigar store late and encountering the events around Marion’s body. Read with the two-building discussion, that account makes Vic’s route and location part of the evening’s sequence.

15 Does Marion’s work and background — from Naval Intelligence to fighting the Alaskan Highway Bill and being backed to establish a third political party — play a role?+

Volume V brings the naval-intelligence background it describes, third-party plans, federal-surveillance interests, and Alaska Highway legislation into its account of political motive. It presents those subjects alongside Marion’s intended dinner speech and the removal of his remarks from the Congressional Record. The volume’s explanation treats his political work and institutional conflicts as central to the case.

16 Is it a coincidence that Illinois statesman John Bolton died suspiciously less than one month before?+

Volume III includes a biographical account of Illinois legislator John M. Bolton’s killing in Chicago on July 9, 1936, following a car chase. That date is 29 days before Marion’s death on August 7. The reproduced material describes contemporary reporting about organized crime, political interests, and competing explanations for Bolton’s killing. The archive places this episode alongside the Zioncheck research as a comparison from the same period.

17 Why are FOIA requests on Marion denied to this day?+

The federal-records discussion in Volume IV calls for FOIA and archival requests concerning surveillance or monitoring of Marion during 1935–1936, tied to his institutional conflicts and interest in federal operations. Volume V also calls for access to federal surveillance files. The supplied PDFs contain these requests for records, but no agency denial letter explaining a present-day refusal.
